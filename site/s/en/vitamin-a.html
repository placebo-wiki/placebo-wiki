Vitamin A: does it work, and how to take it

Vitamin A: verdict: it depends, evidence level C. It may help, but only with a confirmed deficiency; for the healthy without a deficiency no benefit has been shown.

it depends an extract of 11 145 studies

What research shows

Vitamin A may help, but only to correct an established deficiency: it is a basic need of the body.

With food insecurity, micronutrient deficiency, including vitamin A deficiency, is associated in 89% of studies, so vitamin A may help exactly in this group.

What is not proven

It is unknown whether vitamin A helps with androgenetic alopecia: the data on vitamin A in the review are weak, and the emphasis is on vitamins B and D, iron and zinc.

Myths

The myth "more A: better skin and hair" is wrong: in the review on androgenetic alopecia the emphasis is on B, D, iron and zinc, and the role of vitamin A is weak.

The myth "vitamins are always enough" is wrong: with food insecurity deficiency is common.

The myth "A is harmless" is wrong: vitamin A is fat-soluble, and the ESPEN guideline keeps its toxicity in mind.

How to take it

No specific supplement doses are given in the abstracts: the dosage is set according to status and deficiency. It is taken in a course with a confirmed deficiency, for weeks or months, with status monitoring and a repeat assessment (ESPEN 2022); retinol and beta-carotene differ in toxicity. No upper limit has been set, but the vitamin is fat-soluble and toxicity is possible.

Who should be careful

Healthy people without a deficiency do not need vitamin A: no benefit has been shown.

For everyone who does not monitor their vitamin status, high doses are not justified: the vitamin is fat-soluble, it accumulates, and ESPEN warns about potential toxicity.

With medicines

The fact sheet lists no known interactions with medicines, but before taking it with any medicines it is worth consulting a doctor.
