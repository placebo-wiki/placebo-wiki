Iron: does it work, and how to take it

Iron works, but only with a deficiency: in anemia and with low ferritin it is needed, and at a normal level there is no benefit.

works an extract of 5 868 studies

What research shows

In pregnancy iron lowers the risk of anemia: relative risk 0.30 (range 0.20-0.47), and it helps bring ferritin back to normal.

Iron may bring back energy, but only with anemia or a latent deficiency: at normal ferritin there will be no surge of strength.

Restoring iron may improve attention and concentration, but only in people with a deficiency: at normal ferritin there is no effect.

Taking it 1-3 times a week may suit those who tolerate iron poorly: nausea and constipation happen less often than with daily intake.

Myths

Myth: the more iron, the more energy. At normal ferritin the supplement gives no benefit.

Myth: iron is taken only daily. A schedule of 1-3 times a week is easier to tolerate.

Myth: tea fully cancels the effect of iron. Tea only lowers its absorption.

How to take it

The dose is set only by a ferritin blood test and a doctor's prescription. Upper limit: 45 mg per day. Ferritin and hemoglobin rise in 4-12 weeks; a schedule of 1-3 times a week is gentler on the stomach and gut. Chelated forms (bisglycinate) are gentler on the stomach than sulfate; take it with vitamin C, but not together with calcium.

Who should be careful

People with a normal ferritin level hardly need the supplement: the effect depends on the starting level, and constipation and nausea during intake are common.

In pregnancy iron is recommended by the WHO as a routine measure, but the doctor sets the schedule.

With medicines

Proton pump inhibitors: they lower iron absorption, so the intake schedule is agreed with a doctor.

Calcium: it competes with iron for absorption, so they are taken separately.

Levodopa: iron lowers its effect, so joint intake must be reported to the doctor.
