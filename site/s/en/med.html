Copper: does it work, and how to take it

Verdict: unproven. The body needs copper as a mineral, but the benefit of a supplement at a normal level is not shown: it makes sense to take it only with a deficiency confirmed by a test.

unproven an extract of 1 186 studies

What research shows

In a systematic review of the vegan diet no copper deficiency was found even in vegans.

The ESPEN guideline directly notes the lack of intervention studies on copper supplements.

What is not proven

It is unknown whether a copper supplement helps with a hidden deficiency without a test: there are no separate studies in humans.

It is unknown whether copper helps with hair loss: in the review on androgenetic alopecia it is only mentioned in the list of micronutrients.

The effect of a copper supplement outside deficiency is not confirmed.

Myths

The myth "everyone needs copper" is wrong: in a systematic review of the vegan diet no copper deficiency was found.

The myth "copper improves hair growth" is wrong: in the review on androgenetic alopecia the conclusions concern B vitamins, vitamin D, iron, selenium and zinc, and copper is simply in the list.

How to take it

The dose is not indicated in the available studies, and the timing of the appearance of the effect was not studied: there are no separate RCTs with measurement of timing. The upper limit is not indicated in the sources; the ESPEN guideline separately warns of possible toxicity of micronutrients in excess. If a deficiency is suspected, a blood test is needed, not self-prescribed intake.

Who should be careful

People without a deficiency confirmed by a test do not need the supplement: in the ordinary diet no copper deficiency was found even in vegans.

With an excess of micronutrients toxicity is possible, about which the ESPEN guideline warns.

With medicines

There are no known interactions with medicines.
